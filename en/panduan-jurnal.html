Practical guide

Choosing the right journal, and spotting predatory ones

Picking the wrong journal can cost money, time, and reputation all at once. This page explains journal access models, what a major publisher really means, how to read quartiles, and how to spot a predatory journal, using real examples from the work in this showcase.

Four access models

What confuses new authors most is who pays. There are four models, and all four are legitimate. What matters is understanding the consequences before you submit.

ModelWho paysWhat it means for you
Subscription Readers or institutional libraries. The author pays nothing. Free for you, but your article can only be read by people whose institution subscribes. Everyone else has to request a copy directly from you. In this showcase: the IJOA and HESWBL papers, both Emerald.
Open access with no charge
Diamond open access		 The body running the journal, usually a university, a ministry, or a publisher absorbing the cost. The author pays nothing. The combination most authors overlook: anyone can read the article and it still costs you nothing to publish. There are fewer such journals, so you have to look for them deliberately. In this showcase: JWAM from Emerald, JIEB from Universitas Gadjah Mada, and Indonesian Treasury Review from the Ministry of Finance.
Paid open access
Gold open access		 The author or their institution, through an article processing charge known as an APC. Anyone can read and download it, which raises the chance of being read and cited. But the cost is real, ranging from hundreds to thousands of dollars depending on the publisher. In this showcase: Cogent Psychology from Taylor and Francis, the only work here that actually carried a fee.
Hybrid A subscription journal that offers the option of paying to make a single article open. The choice is yours. Without payment the article stays behind the subscription. With payment, your article alone becomes open. In this showcase: the Evidence-based HRM paper, published through the subscription route and therefore free of charge.

The belief that stops most people before they even try is that a reputable international journal must be expensive. The numbers in this showcase say otherwise. Of the seven works displayed, six Scopus papers and one SINTA journal, only one actually incurred an article processing charge, and that was Cogent Psychology. The other six cost nothing. Two went through the subscription route, one through the hybrid route without paying, and three were fully open access journals that charge no fee at all.

That third group deserves the emphasis. Many authors assume open access automatically means paying, and rule it out from the start. In reality there is a set of journals whose costs are already covered by the institution behind them, so the article is open to everyone while the author is asked for nothing. These journals rarely sit at the top of a search result, so they have to be sought out on purpose. Once found, they offer both things at once: wide readership and zero cost.

One further point is widely misunderstood and worth stating plainly: paying an APC is not buying acceptance. At a proper journal the charge only arises after the manuscript has been accepted through genuine review. If a journal asks for money upfront as a condition of processing, or promises acceptance in exchange for payment, that is a warning sign of the kind discussed below.

What a major publisher actually means

People often call them tier one publishers. The term is informal, but it refers to long-established international academic publishing houses that run hundreds or thousands of journals.

They include Elsevier, Springer Nature, Wiley, Taylor and Francis, Sage, and Emerald. Publishing with them is something to be proud of, because their review systems are well established, their editing is thorough, and their readership is wide.

But there is an important point rarely explained to new authors. A big publisher name is no guarantee of the quality of the specific journal you are targeting. One major publisher can house a Q1 journal and a Q4 journal at the same time. What determines the weight of your publication is the standing of the journal itself, not the logo on its cover.

So it helps to reverse the order of your checks. Do not start by asking who publishes it. Start by asking whether the journal is genuinely indexed, in which quartile it sits, and whether its scope fits your manuscript. The publisher name is the last piece of information you need, not the first.

Reading Scimago quartiles

A quartile groups journals by their rank within a subject field. Every journal in the field is ranked, then split into four. Q1 means the journal sits in the top quarter, Q2 in the next, and so on down to Q4.

Two things are commonly misunderstood. First, one journal can hold different quartiles in different fields. The same title might be Q1 under management and Q2 under psychology. If you quote a quartile, quote the category with it. Second, quartiles change every year as the figures are recalculated. A Q1 journal this year can slip to Q2 next year, and the reverse also happens.

Checking is free and open: go to Scimago Journal Rank at scimagojr.com, type the journal name, and look at its category and quartile. Do not rely on whatever the journal claims on its own site.

Ten signs of a predatory journal

Predatory journals collect fees without running genuine review. The damage is not only the money lost, but an article recorded somewhere that actively harms your track record. The more of these signs appear, the stronger the reason to walk away.

1

Flattering emails out of the blue

An email arrives praising your work in language that is far too sweet, often naming a field that is not even yours, or misspelling your name. Reputable journals do not chase authors through mass email.

2

Promises of very fast publication

Publication promised within days, or one to two weeks. Genuine peer review takes time. For comparison, one paper in this showcase was accepted in five weeks and that counts as very fast, while another needed five rounds across nearly a year.

3

Fees that are not transparent

The amount is not stated clearly on the site, or is only mentioned once the manuscript has been accepted. A proper journal publishes its fees openly from the start.

4

A scope that is far too broad

A single journal claiming to accept medicine, engineering, economics, and literature all at once. Real journals have clear disciplinary boundaries because their editorial board actually works in that field.

5

An editorial board you cannot verify

Editor names with no affiliation, no institutional email, or who turn out never to have agreed to serve. Some predatory journals list academics without permission.

6

False indexing claims

Displaying Scopus, Web of Science, or DOAJ logos when the journal does not appear in those official lists. A logo on a journal website proves nothing. Always check from the indexing body’s side.

7

No real review process

The manuscript is accepted with not a single request for change. That sounds pleasant, but it is precisely what should worry you. Genuine review almost always produces revision requests, however small.

8

A shoddy website

Frequent typos, broken layout, dead links, no verifiable office address, or a free email account listed as the official editorial contact.

9

A name that mimics a well-known journal

Deliberately close to the name of a reputable title, sometimes differing by a single word or the addition of international. Check the ISSN and the publisher, not just the name.

10

Difficulty withdrawing your manuscript

When you want to withdraw a submission, the journal makes it hard, charges a withdrawal fee, or simply stops replying. A proper journal respects an author’s right to withdraw.

Four steps to verify a journal

Do all four before you submit. Every one is free and the whole thing takes under ten minutes.

Check the quartile and indexing at the source

Open scimagojr.com and search the journal name. If it is not there, the journal is not Scopus indexed, whatever its website claims.

For open access journals, check DOAJ

The Directory of Open Access Journals at doaj.org screens open access titles that run genuine review. Being listed there is a good sign.

Trace the editorial board

Take two or three editor names, search for them, and confirm their affiliations are real and that they genuinely work in that field. If in doubt, send a short email to check.

Use the Think Check Submit checklist

Visit thinkchecksubmit.org, a cross-industry initiative offering a concise checklist for judging whether a journal is trustworthy.

National journals are not second class

There is a mistaken assumption worth correcting: that a journal not indexed in Scopus is automatically worth less. That is not true. The danger is predatory journals, not national ones.

Indonesia runs its own accreditation system called SINTA, graded from SINTA 1 to SINTA 6. Journals at SINTA 1 and 2 run serious peer review, have a clear editorial board, and publish on schedule. This showcase includes one: Indonesian Treasury Review, run by the Ministry of Finance and rated SINTA 2.

A good national journal plays three roles nothing else can fill. First, a training ground. Writing for an accredited national journal builds the same discipline as an international one, under more manageable pressure. Second, reaching the right readers. Research on Indonesian policy often lands harder when Indonesian policymakers read it, and they read national journals. Third, meeting specific career requirements that call for nationally accredited publication.

So the question is not national or international. It is whether the journal runs genuine review. That is the only dividing line that truly matters.
