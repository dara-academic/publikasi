Effectiveness analysis of Nadine implementation at Indonesia's MoF: a combined approach using importance-performance analysis and causal relationship analysis

Despinur Dara (first author, Universitas Negeri Jakarta) and Donny Maha Putra (corresponding author, Universitas Pembangunan Nasional Veteran Jakarta).

In brief

      
This paper evaluates the effectiveness of Nadine, the electronic official correspondence application implemented by the Indonesian Ministry of Finance. Drawing on data from more than 2,500 employees, the study measures user satisfaction, identifies areas for improvement, and tests the relationship between policy success and application performance. The conclusion is encouraging and demanding at once: satisfaction is high, but the interface and navigation still need work.

      
The interesting lesson: the success of a digital policy and the performance of the application delivering it are tightly correlated. Improving the application is not merely a technical matter; it lifts the effectiveness of the policy along with it.


      
Background

      
Digital transformation has become a government agenda worldwide, with Estonia and South Korea the success stories most often cited. Indonesia faces a different challenge: infrastructure readiness and digital literacy remain uneven, and only around 65 per cent of civil servants are able to operate digital applications. Against that background the Ministry of Finance introduced Nadine to replace manual official correspondence with a system that is faster, more efficient, and more secure. The evaluative question is simple but consequential: how effective is this system in the eyes of its own users, and how does that relate to the success of the policy behind it?


      
Research gap

      
The paper fills two gaps at once. First, public sector digitalisation research is dominated by developed-country contexts with far better infrastructure and digital literacy, so the challenges particular to developing countries are under-represented. Second, and this is less often noticed, the literature tends to overlook the impact of digitalisation policy at the micro-organisational level of a single ministry or agency. This paper enters precisely that level, with one concrete application inside one ministry.


      
Novelty

      
Its distinctiveness lies in an empirical examination of technology acceptance within the Indonesian public administration at a data scale rarely achieved, plus a combination of methods rarely used together: importance-performance analysis to map improvement priorities into four quadrants, alongside correlation and regression analysis to test the link between policy success and application performance. The output is not merely a satisfaction score but an action map that system managers can use directly.


      
Theoretical landscape

      
Two technology adoption theories are combined. Rogers innovation diffusion theory contributes five characteristics that influence the adoption of an innovation: relative advantage, compatibility, complexity, trialability, and observability, each translated into concrete question items about Nadine. Venkatesh UTAUT completes the picture with four behavioural constructs: performance expectancy, effort expectancy, social influence, and facilitating conditions. Together they allow the paper to capture individual and organisational factors at once, which matters in a hierarchical bureaucratic environment.


      
Method

      
A quantitative survey of 2,551 Ministry of Finance employees across a range of work units, selected purposively so that only those with genuine experience of Nadine were captured. Representation is broad: the Directorate General of State Assets at 36 per cent, the Secretariat General at 21 per cent, and the remainder spread across Customs and Excise, Treasury, Taxation, Budgeting, and other units, with respondents over 45 forming the largest age group. The instrument was divided into two families, policy aspects and application aspects, with question order randomised to suppress order bias and anonymity guaranteed to encourage candid answers.


      
Findings

      
The user satisfaction index is high and consistent: 4.70 for policy and 4.67 for the application on a five-point scale, falling into the excellent category across almost all regions of Indonesia from Sumatra to Papua, with Jakarta slightly below.

      
But the quadrant mapping is the most useful output. Service development features and ease of use were rated highly important yet not yet satisfactory in performance, which makes them the first priority for improvement. Conversely, ease of access, processing speed, and support for a paperless culture were already satisfactory and should be maintained. On the policy side, users still perceive a gap in understanding the objectives and the standardisation of official correspondence, while the smoothness of written communication has become a strength.

      
The most strategic finding is the correlation between the policy aspect and the application aspect, reaching 0.859, which counts as very strong, with a coefficient of determination of roughly 73.79 per cent. Policy and the system that carries it are not two separate concerns but one whole in which each determines the other.


      
Contribution

      
For government practitioners the recommendations are concrete: fix the interface and navigation first, because that is where the largest gap sits between expectation and reality; run more workshops to raise user understanding; prepare educational material; and maintain a continuous feedback channel. Resource allocation can also be shifted away from areas already performing well but of lower importance towards the areas that are critical.

      
For academics, the paper adds empirical evidence for innovation diffusion theory and UTAUT from a developing-country bureaucratic context, with a message that generalises: improving system quality is a policy investment, not merely an IT matter.

        What this timeline teaches three things
        

          
First, the authors answered very fast, and that cut the total time. The first round outcome came on 28 August and the revision went back on 31 August, three days. The second round came on 7 October and the revision went in on 9 October, two days. Of roughly four and a half months in total, less than a week was actually spent on the author side. The rest was waiting, which is outside an author control.

          
Second, there is a formal proofreading stage that many journals do not have. The managing staff asked the author to confirm willingness to go through proofreading on 22 July, well before any review outcome. The actual proofreading request came on 25 October and the result went back on 2 November. It is a stage of its own before copyediting, and for an English-language manuscript written by Indonesian authors it helps considerably.

          
Third, accepted does not mean immediately published. Copyediting concluded on 20 November 2024, but the article only appeared in Vol. 10 No. 1 of 2025. That gap between finishing and appearing is normal, and needs allowing for if you are working to a reporting deadline.

        

      



Unlike the international journals in this showcase, which use narrative decision letters, Indonesian Treasury Review uses a structured peer review form. The reviewer fills three boxes: summary, major issues, and minor issues. The format makes the feedback easier to act on, because every note already points to the page that needs fixing.


      
One thing is worth noting before the content. The title of this manuscript changed during review. It went in as "Optimizing digital transformation: evaluating the effectiveness of the Nadine system at Indonesia's MoF" and was published under a far more specific title naming the methods. This is the same pattern as the JWAM paper here. A title is not sacred, and naming the method in it makes the article easier to find for people looking for a similar approach.


      

        Summary assessment positive in tone
        

          
The reviewer judged the article comprehensive and broadly compliant with scientific writing conventions, with some room for improvement. One note that Indonesian authors writing in English easily forget: because the article is entirely in English, the reviewer asked for a proof read to ensure the writing quality matches the language chosen.

        

      


      

        Three major issues all about the reasoning behind each choice
        

          
Tellingly, all three main notes point the same way. They do not question the results; they ask for the reasoning behind every choice the authors made. This is a complaint that recurs across every review document in this showcase.

          
Literature review, page 2. The reviewer asked for an explanation of why the authors used Innovation Diffusion Theory and UTAUT, stating openly that such an explanation would show the authors had read enough of the literature before choosing. Naming a theory is not enough; it has to be clear why that theory and not another.

          
Population and sample, page 6. The authors were asked to explain why that sampling method was chosen over others. Again, not questioning the sample but asking for the reasoning.

          
Analysis techniques, page 6. The reviewer asked for detail on how the online survey was administered, so that the adequacy of bias control could be judged and the results shown to remain objective. For a survey of 2,551 employees, that is a fair and important question.

        

      


      

        Minor issues none
        

          
The minor issues box carried two words: none detected. No problems with ambiguous wording, figures, units of analysis, tables, images, or citation. For an article of this length, an empty minor box signals a manuscript that arrived already tidy.

        

      


      

        What the comparison teaches national versus international
        

          
Set this alongside what the Scopus papers in the same showcase received. At IJOA one reviewer recommended rejection and wrote that the Stoic books they were reading were easier to follow. At Evidence-based HRM one stated there was no path forward unless sample representativeness was proven. At JIEB one rebuked the writing style for reading like the output of an AI application.

          
Here the tone is far calmer, the minor issues box is empty, and all three main notes can be answered by adding explanation rather than rebuilding a model or rerunning an analysis.

          
That difference does not make national journals less serious. What differs is the level of difficulty and pressure, and that is precisely why an accredited national journal is a good place to practise. You go through genuine review and receive specific, page-referenced notes, but under pressure that remains manageable. Once answering notes like these becomes familiar, notes from international journals start to feel reasonable.
