Writing guide

Writing a manuscript section by section, from what reviewers actually ask for

Other pages here show what reviewers criticised. This page answers the next question: how to write so that the criticism does not land. Everything below is drawn from 25 real reviewer comments on seven published works, not from a handbook.

Introduction Research gap Literature review Method Results Discussion Conclusion, limitations, and implications Administrative matters that hold a paper back

One pattern runs through almost every review document. Reviewers rarely dispute your results. What they most often demand is the reasoning behind every choice you made: why that theory, why that method, why those respondents, why compared against those studies. If you only have time to fix one thing, fix that.

1

Introduction

The most-read section, and the one most often written last.

There is already a recipe for the structure

Reviewers rarely demand an elegant introduction. What they demand is a structure they can follow.

What you can do

Write the introduction in four paragraphs. One to introduce the topic and its key terms, one to show what the literature has not answered, one to state the problem, one to state the research objectives. If any of those is missing, a reviewer will ask for it.

Do not write at a distance from the reader

Circling sentences and stacked terminology make a reviewer lose the thread, and they will say so plainly.

What you can do

Once written, read your introduction aloud. A sentence that leaves you short of breath will almost certainly stop a reviewer. Place one real example as early as you can, because an example makes an abstract idea immediately visible.

2

Research gap

The shortest section, and the one most often judged not yet convincing.

A different context alone is not a gap

Saying that earlier studies looked elsewhere is not a persuasive reason for a new study.

What you can do

Explain why that difference in context changes how the phenomenon works, not merely where it sits. If the public sector carries different authority structures, incentives, or pressures, say what changes because of them. A strong gap always reads as this theory has not been tested under theoretically different conditions, not simply not tested here.

3

Literature review

Often written as a list when what is asked for is a judgement.

Weigh, do not summarise

Arranging earlier studies in sequence is not treated as a critical review.

What you can do

For every group of studies you cite, add one sentence of judgement. What they have established, what remains open, and why that matters for your study. Include findings that run against your hypothesis, because a review carrying only supportive work reads as advocacy rather than review.

Explain why that theory and not another

Naming a theory is not enough. Reviewers want to know why it was chosen.

What you can do

Write one short paragraph naming two or three alternative theories commonly used for similar problems, then explain what those cannot account for and yours can. That paragraph doubles as evidence that you read widely.

Keep the writing voice your own

Reviewers are now alert to prose that reads generic and authorless.

What you can do

Prose that is too tidy, too balanced, and carries no opinion is itself suspicious. Show your own judgement. Say which studies you find most convincing and which are weak, and why.

4

Method

The section that attracts the most questions, and the questions are always similar.

Five questions you will almost certainly be asked

Reviewers judge a method section by whether someone could repeat your study from it alone.

What you can do

Answer all five in the manuscript before being asked. State the formula or reasoning behind sample size, the channels used to reach respondents, how bias was suppressed including any anonymisation, the nature of the data, and the collection window with its rationale.

For qualitative work the questions get finer still

Credibility in qualitative research rests on procedural transparency, not on the number of informants.

What you can do

Include the interview guide as an appendix with the rationale for each question. State who interviewed and what their position was relative to the informants. Say when thematic saturation was reached. A small sample is not a problem so long as the procedure is open.

Statistics have to be traceable

A quantitative reviewer will check the plausibility of your numbers one by one.

What you can do

Report skewness and kurtosis, not just mean and standard deviation. Name the software and approach with your reasoning. If a methodological decision was forced by non-normal data, state it openly, because doing so strengthens the paper.

5

Results

The section most often blended into the discussion.

Separate it from the discussion

Results present what was found. The discussion explains what it means. The two must not merge.

What you can do

Create two distinct sections with their own headings. In results, present figures and findings without interpretation. Interpretation, comparison, and implications all belong in the discussion.

Surprising findings need the longest explanation

A result that contradicts your expectation is not a weakness, but it must be explained.

What you can do

If the direction you found runs against your hypothesis, do not label it non-significant and move on. Explain a plausible mechanism behind it. Unexpected findings explained well often become the strongest part of a paper.

6

Discussion

The single most common weak point across every review document in this showcase.

Not a restatement of results, and not two references

The discussion is where your findings are set against the findings of others.

What you can do

For each main finding, cite at least two other studies, one that agrees and one that differs, then explain why your result came out as it did. Do not avoid the disagreement. A difference you explain shows command of the literature.

Address alternative explanations and boundary conditions

A reviewer will think of other explanations for your findings. Better that you name them first.

What you can do

Add a short section on the conditions under which your findings may not hold. This does not weaken the paper; it shows you know the limits of your own claim.

7

Conclusion, limitations, and implications

Often written in a rush against a deadline, and it shows.

Limitations stand alone, and their placement is arguable

The section is compulsory, but reviewers can disagree about where it belongs.

What you can do

Pick one placement and explain your reasoning in the cover letter. When reviewers contradict each other you are not obliged to satisfy both. Editors value a reasoned decision over blind compliance.

Implications must be actionable

An implication reading organisations should pay attention is not treated as an implication.

What you can do

Write implications as actions that can be taken, not as exhortations. Say who should do what. Where possible, give a threshold or measure that can be monitored.

8

Administrative matters that hold a paper back

Nothing to do with research quality, and still capable of sinking a submission.

A research ethics statement is not a formality

Some journals refuse to publish manuscripts that cannot evidence it.

What you can do

Prepare this while writing the method section, not when it is demanded during revision. If your institution has no ethics committee, state compliance with the applicable policy and how respondent consent was obtained.

Word limits are often counted differently from how you assume

A rule almost nobody reads, which once nearly sank a paper in this showcase.

What you can do

Read the author guidelines to the end before submitting, not after being told. Check the word limit, how it is counted including whether tables and figures count, the reference format, and any anonymity requirement.

English-language manuscripts need a more fluent reader

A common request for Indonesian authors writing in English.

What you can do

Set aside time and budget for language checking before submitting. Halting language makes reviewers doubt the care taken elsewhere, even when the substance is strong.
