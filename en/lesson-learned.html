Eight lessons from the reviewers desk

Real lessons from six published works: how to read reviewer comments, how to respond when a manuscript is recommended for rejection, and how long the process actually takes. All of it drawn from the original documents rather than from a handbook.

What almost nobody publishes, published here

The anatomy of each paper

Reviewer comments, unedited

Honest timelines

The eight lessons

Every item below comes from an actual review document. Follow the link at the end of each one to read the full breakdown of that paper.

1. A recommendation to reject is not the end of the story

On the burnout latency paper, one reviewer openly recommended rejection with a painful critique, saying the argument had become lost in the complexity of its own language. The editor decided otherwise, treating that recommendation as a major revision and inviting the authors to revise. The paper is now published in a Q1 journal. As long as the editor keeps the door open, do not rush to give up. Read the breakdown

2. The harshest critique usually points at the realest weakness

The same reviewer complained that the manuscript never touched the ground because it offered no concrete examples. The answer was not to argue back but to add narrative vignettes about employees who look fine while quietly running on empty. That section became one of the most readable parts of the paper. A critique that stings is often a critique that is right. Read the breakdown

3. Review speed varies enormously between journals

The hybrid work paper in the Journal of Work-Applied Management was accepted just five weeks after submission. The paper on young lecturers in JIEB needed five review rounds spanning January to July. Both are solid pieces of work. The length of the process reflects reviewer availability and journal policy, not the quality of the manuscript. Read the breakdown

4. The discussion section is the most common weak point

A JIEB reviewer described a discussion resting on only one or two references as a fundamental weakness, and called it worrying. A discussion is not a repeat of the results, it is where your findings are set against other people’s. Plan that section early rather than writing it in a rush before submission. Read the breakdown

5. Reviewers can contradict each other, and that is normal

At Cogent Psychology, one reviewer asked for the limitations section to come before the conclusion, while another asked for it to come after. Authors are not obliged to satisfy both. Pick one, then explain the choice in the cover letter. Editors respect a reasoned decision more than blind compliance. Read the breakdown

6. Even the title gets revised

The JWAM manuscript arrived under a different title from the one it carries in print, and it changed on the reviewers advice. Many authors treat the title as sacred. In truth it is the reader’s front door, and reviewers often see it more clearly than the author does. Read the breakdown

7. An ethics statement is not a formality

The editor of Higher Education, Skills and Work-Based Learning stated plainly that the journal does not publish work which cannot evidence adherence to institutional ethics policies. Prepare the ethical review statement while you are still writing the method, rather than waiting to be asked. Read the breakdown

8. A small sample is no barrier to a Q1 journal

A qualitative study with ten informants was published in a Q1 journal. Reviewers did flag the sample as a limitation, but the manuscript still passed because its contribution claims were proportionate and its limitations were acknowledged openly. What is non-negotiable is not sample size, it is clarity of theory and transparency of procedure. Read the breakdown
