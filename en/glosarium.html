Glossary

The publishing terms that confuse people, explained one by one

Many first-time authors stall not because the research is weak but because the vocabulary journals use is unfamiliar, and asking feels embarrassing. This page explains 40 of the terms that come up most, in plain language and with real examples from the work in this showcase.

40 terms

Submitting the manuscript The review process After acceptance Rankings and indexes Cost and access Ethics and integrity

No term matches that. Try a shorter keyword, or send the term by email so it can be added.

Submitting the manuscript

The terms that appear from the moment you press submit until the paper formally enters the review queue.

Manuscript

Your paper while it is still in process and not yet published. As long as it is a manuscript, it cannot be listed as a publication. Only once it is accepted and appears online does it become an article.

Corresponding author

The one person on the author list designated as the official contact with the journal. Every decision letter, revision request, and proof goes to their inbox. The role is administrative rather than a marker of who contributed most, although many Indonesian universities do count it in assessment. Agree on it early so it does not become a dispute mid process.

Cover letter

A short letter to the editor explaining why your manuscript suits that particular journal. It is not a restatement of the abstract. What the editor looks for is why this work matters to their readership, plus confirmation that the manuscript is not published or under review elsewhere.

Aims and scope

The journal official statement of which topics it accepts. This is the section authors most often skip, and the most common reason a manuscript is rejected without review. Five minutes reading it can save four months of waiting.

Manuscript ID

The code the system assigns on submission, for example EBHRM-10-2025-0465. It stays with the paper until publication and gains the suffix R1, R2 and so on with each revision you submit. Keep it, because all correspondence refers to it.

Submission systemScholarOne, Editorial Manager

The online platform where the manuscript is uploaded and the whole process is tracked. Emerald uses ScholarOne, Elsevier uses Editorial Manager, and Taylor and Francis runs its own. All of them show live status, so there is no need to email an editor merely to ask where the paper stands.

The review process

The most nerve-racking part of publishing and the part least often described. Every term below will appear in an editor decision letter.

Desk reject

The manuscript is rejected by the editor without ever reaching a reviewer, usually within days to two weeks. The usual cause is not weak research but a topic outside scope, language not yet readable, or formatting that ignores the guidelines. The upside is speed: you can move the paper elsewhere immediately.

Peer review

Assessment of the manuscript by fellow researchers in the same field, usually two to four of them, working unpaid. They judge whether the method holds, the findings are plausible, and the contribution is new enough. This is what separates a scholarly journal from ordinary publishing.

Double-blind review

Reviewers do not know who the authors are, and authors do not know who reviewed. The manuscript therefore has to be stripped of names, affiliations, and self-citations that would reveal identity. It is the most common model in the social sciences and management.

Editor in chief and associate editor

The editor in chief holds the final decision and signs the acceptance letter. The associate editor manages a single manuscript end to end, selects reviewers, and drafts the recommendation. On the Evidence-based HRM paper in this showcase, the editor in chief was Prof. Thomas Lange and the associate editor Prof. Fabian Homberg.

Major revision

The manuscript is judged promising but with fundamental issues to resolve, typically in method, theoretical framing, or sample representativeness. It is not a rejection. The paper will be reassessed after you revise. Receiving major revision twice in a row is still normal, and that is exactly what happened to the Evidence-based HRM paper here before it was accepted.

Minor revision

The substance is accepted in principle and only small fixes remain, such as clarity, added explanation, or tidier referencing. It usually goes back to the editor rather than the reviewers. Reaching this stage makes acceptance very likely.

Revise and resubmit

A heavier form of major revision. The original submission is formally closed and your revised work is treated as a new manuscript with the review process starting over. It is used when the changes required are very large.

Response to reviewer

A separate document answering every reviewer comment one by one, stating where in the paper each change can be found. On the second round this document is often more decisive than the manuscript itself. You are allowed to disagree with a reviewer, provided the reasoning is courteous and grounded.

RoundR1, R2, R3

The revision cycle. R1 is the first revised version, R2 the second, and so on. Reputable journals typically run two to three rounds. The number of rounds measures the thoroughness of the journal, not the weakness of the paper.

Reject

The manuscript will not proceed at that journal. Worth knowing: one reviewer recommending rejection does not automatically mean rejection. On the International Journal of Organizational Analysis paper in this showcase, one reviewer recommended rejection in round one, and the paper was still published after revision.

After acceptance

Accepted does not yet mean published, and the gap between the two often surprises first-time authors.

Accepted

The formal decision that the manuscript will be published. From this point the content no longer changes. Some universities already treat the acceptance letter as evidence of publication, but the article itself is not yet readable by anyone.

Proof

The typeset version of the paper in the journal layout, sent to you for a final check. Only typographical errors, numbers, and name spellings may be corrected. It is not an opportunity to add discussion. The deadline is usually short, two to three days.

Online first, early access

The article is readable and citable through its DOI even before it is assigned to a specific issue. Practical for reporting requirements, because the article formally exists.

Volume and issue

Markers of where the article sits within the journal run. Volume generally marks the year, issue the number within that year. Both are needed for a complete citation.

DOIDigital Object Identifier

The permanent code that serves as the fixed address of your article, for example 10.1108/EBHRM-10-2025-0465. Unlike an ordinary link that can break, a DOI is designed to hold indefinitely. Always include it in your reference list.

Rankings and indexes

The numbers that determine how a publication is judged, and the ones most often misunderstood.

Scopus

A database that lists scholarly journals worldwide after a selection process. Scopus-indexed means the journal passed that selection, not that your article is automatically of high quality. Scopus can also remove a journal from its list, so check current status before submitting.

SINTA

The Indonesian ministry ranking system for national journals, tiered from SINTA 1 down to SINTA 6. SINTA 1 and 2 correspond to strong national journals. A national journal is not second class; it is a place to practise good writing alongside pursuing international ones.

QuartileQ1, Q2, Q3, Q4

The division of journals within a field into four groups by citation performance. Q1 is the top 25 per cent, Q4 the bottom. One journal can sit in different quartiles in different subject fields, so always state which field you mean. Quartiles also change annually.

Scimago Journal RankSJR

A measure of journal influence that counts not just how many citations but which journals they come from. Citations from prestigious journals weigh more. The quartiles commonly cited in Indonesia generally refer to this Scimago ranking.

Impact factor

The average number of citations to a journal articles over the preceding two years, published by Clarivate for journals in Web of Science. Not every Scopus journal has an impact factor, because the two are different systems. Do not conflate them.

CiteScore

Scopus own citation measure, calculated across a four-year window. The longer window makes it more stable than the impact factor.

h-index

A measure attached to a researcher, not a journal. An h of 10 means you have 10 articles each cited at least 10 times. It can only rise and moves slowly, so it is a poor yardstick early in a career.

Cost and access

The part that most often stops people before they try, and the part most widely misunderstood.

Article processing chargeAPC

The fee an author pays so the article is open to everyone. It runs from hundreds to thousands of dollars depending on the publisher. Crucially, paying it is not buying acceptance. At a proper journal the charge only arises after the manuscript is accepted. Of the seven works in this showcase, only one incurred it.

Open access

The article can be read and downloaded by anyone without a subscription. Worth remembering: open access does not always mean the author pays. Some journals have their costs covered by the institution behind them.

Diamond open access

A journal that is open to readers and free for authors, because the cost is absorbed by a university, a ministry, or the publisher. It is the best of both and the most overlooked, and because there are fewer of them they have to be sought out deliberately. Three works in this showcase were published this way.

Subscription

The older model where readers or libraries pay and the author pays nothing. The drawback is that your article is only readable by those whose institution subscribes, so everyone else has to request a copy from you.

Hybrid

A subscription journal offering the option of paying to open a single article. Without payment the article still publishes behind the subscription at no cost at all.

Preprint

A manuscript posted to an open repository before peer review. Useful for staking a claim to an idea, but check the target journal policy because some treat it as prior publication.

Ethics and integrity

Rarely taught, and the most expensive thing to get wrong.

Predatory journal

A journal that charges a fee but runs no genuine peer review. Warning signs include soliciting by email, promising publication within days, asking for money before the manuscript is assessed, and listing an editorial board that cannot be verified. Publishing there is not recognised, and the trace is hard to erase.

Similarity check

The percentage of your manuscript matching other text, usually measured with Turnitin. Many journals require under 15 to 20 per cent. Even a low figure is a problem if the overlap sits in the core of your argument.

Salami slicing

Splitting one study into several small articles to inflate publication count. It is detectable because the samples and methods are near identical, and it can end in retraction.

Authorship order

The sequence of names on the article. The first author is generally the largest contributor. Agree the order in writing at the outset, because changing it after submission is difficult and some journals refuse it.

Conflict of interest

A statement of any interest that could influence the research, such as funding from a party that benefits from your findings. Declaring it does not get a paper rejected. Concealing it is what causes damage.

Retraction

Withdrawal of a published article because of serious error or ethical breach. The article stays visible but marked as retracted, and the record stays attached to the author name permanently.
