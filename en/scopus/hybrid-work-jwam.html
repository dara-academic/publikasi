Hybrid work in the public sector: practical insights on organizational support

Despinur Dara (penulis pertama), Naura Yasmin Zahraini, dan Resi Rejalia, Fakultas Ekonomi dan Bisnis, Universitas Negeri Jakarta.

In brief

      
Most hybrid work studies are busy measuring productivity. This paper takes a different road: it explores how Indonesian civil servants interpret and construct the meaning of hybrid work, emphasising its symbolic, intersubjective, and contextual dimensions. The result shows that organisational support is not merely formal policy but a meaning built jointly through institutional rhythms and everyday expectations.

      
The interesting lesson: a qualitative paper with ten informants can reach a Q1 journal, provided the depth of interpretation and the theoretical positioning are strong. Sample size is not everything.


      
Background

      
Hybrid work in the Indonesian bureaucracy is not a talking point but official policy. The Ministry of Administrative and Bureaucratic Reform institutionalised it through Circular Number 24 of 2021, and a 2022 civil service agency survey recorded that 95.7 per cent of civil servants believed hybrid work could improve flexibility and service delivery. Behind that optimism, however, implementation is full of friction: cross-unit coordination stalls, digital workload accumulates, performance evaluation criteria are vague, and access to technology is uneven. The same policy is experienced very differently by different people.


      
Research gap

      
Hybrid work research in Indonesia, including the Cogent Psychology paper elsewhere in this showcase, generally tests causal relationships between hybrid work, productivity, and psychological outcomes. What escapes those causal models is subjective experience: how civil servants interpret flexibility, read the sincerity of support, and negotiate role expectations within their organisational context. That meaning-making process can only be captured phenomenologically, and that is where this paper stands.


      
Novelty

      
The contribution is stated explicitly in three layers: it extends perceived organisational support theory by showing that symbolic and material support are interpreted differently inside a hybrid bureaucracy; it complements the job demands-resources model with phenomenological insight into how demands and resources are experienced subjectively; and it brings empirical evidence from Indonesia, a setting under-represented in the literature. Tellingly, the paper key finding grows from a distinction that is simple but sharp: support that is spoken versus support that is delivered.


      
Theoretical landscape

      
The foundation is Alfred Schutz social phenomenology. The concepts of stock of knowledge, typification, and intersubjectivity are used to read how civil servants construct the meaning of hybrid work from their experience and institutional norms. Schutz separates the realm of pure perception, meaning direct experience of handling digital tasks, from the realm of pure symbol, meaning what is shaped by social interaction and organisational culture. That interpretive frame is then integrated with two mainstream theories, perceived organisational support and job demands-resources, so that structure and meaning can be analysed within one layered model. POS and JD-R capture the objective conditions; Schutz captures how those conditions are lived.


      
Method

      
Ten civil servants were selected purposively against strict criteria: between six months and one year of hybrid working, in roles and levels of involvement relevant to the question. Data were gathered through semi-structured in-depth interviews, with the guide derived from the POS and JD-R frameworks, participatory observation in both virtual and face-to-face meetings, and analysis of policy documents including the 2021 circular. Thematic analysis followed Braun and Clarke through a Schutzian lens, managed in ATLAS.ti.

      
Credibility was protected in three layers: triangulation across three data sources, member checking with informants, and an audit trail. Thematic saturation was reached at the eighth to ninth interview, evidence that ten informants were sufficient for phenomenological analysis.


      
Findings

      
Four interrelated themes emerged, and the informant voices are pointed. On flexibility, one informant felt more productive at home while another chose the office because that is where the data lived: the same phenomenon typified differently depending on each person biographical situation.

      
On organisational support came the most striking dissonance. One informant said a simple thank you meant a great deal but there was no material support at all. Another described everything being left to personal initiative, with no internet allowance and no clear policy.

      
The third theme is the erosion of time boundaries: WhatsApp messages late at night, Zoom meetings outside working hours, and no switch to stop working. The fourth is unsteady coordination: the technology exists but the rules of engagement do not, so collaboration loses its rhythm.

      
The discussion adds a distinctly Indonesian layer. The culture of ewuh pakewuh, a reluctance to confront superiors, makes symbolic recognition feel more valuable while simultaneously preventing employees from voicing unmet resource needs. The gap between policy intent and ground reality therefore persists.


      
Contribution

      
Theoretically, the paper shows that appreciation only carries motivational meaning when it aligns with collective expectation. Verbal praise unsupported by structural reinforcement is read as performative and erodes trust instead.

      
Practically, four recommendations can be executed by an agency straight away: clear working-hours and performance-evaluation policy; inclusive and evenly distributed technology infrastructure; mechanisms for support that is both symbolic and material, covering devices, connectivity, and allowances; and structured communication protocols that bridge remote and office-based staff.

One detail first-time authors rarely notice: the title changed during review. The manuscript went in as "Making Sense of Organizational Support in the Hybrid Work Era: Lessons from the Public Sector" and was sharpened on reviewer advice into its present form. A title is not sacred; it gets revised like any other part of the manuscript.


      

        Reviewer 1 substantive revision, highly detailed
        

          
The first reviewer appreciated the structure and the argument but demanded sharpening at the most central point: the Schutzian phenomenological framework. They asked for its articulation to be clarified, chased a Schutz-Husserl figure whose source was missing from the reference list, and instructed the authors to return to the original sources of JD-R and POS so the theoretical evaluation would carry more force. Context was flagged too: do not use an Australian-context article to support an argument about Indonesia, and strengthen the articulation of the Indonesian public sector setting.

          
On method the questions were concrete, and worth noting by anyone writing qualitative research: was there a pilot interview; what does selecting participants in line with the research aim mean operationally; were interviews online or face to face and what are the trade-offs; who conducted them and how was researcher positionality handled; and what analysis was applied to the observations and policy documents. The reviewer also pointed to a mismatch between the contribution promised in the abstract and the one concluded at the end, suggested adding a framework at the close of the discussion, and proposed changing the title, including removing the phrase "civil servants" as unnecessary.

        

      


      

        Reviewer 2 positive, with a note on sample size
        

          
The second reviewer was far more concise. The writing was judged clear and compact, the theory apt and well connected, and the practical implications called the paper greatest strength because organisational leaders could act on them directly. The main reservation is classic for qualitative research: ten informants was considered small and limiting for generalisation, and the reviewer wanted more participant demographics such as age, gender, and job type.

          
The lesson: a small sample is not a barrier to a Q1 journal so long as the contribution claimed is proportionate and the limitations are acknowledged openly. What is non-negotiable is clarity of theoretical framing and transparency of procedure.
