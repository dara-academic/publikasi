Burnout latency in AI-driven work systems: modelling the silent interval between technostress and burnout

Despinur Dara (first author, corresponding author) dan Dewi Susita, Fakultas Ekonomi dan Bisnis, Universitas Negeri Jakarta.

In brief

      
This paper introduces a new concept called burnout latency: the silent phase in AI-supported work when a person still produces output as usual while focus, energy, and emotional capacity are quietly declining. The concept answers a puzzle organisations increasingly face, namely that burnout complaints keep rising precisely when productivity figures look stable.

      
One sentence carries it: burnout is not a sudden event but the peak of a long process, most of whose phases are invisible to output-based monitoring systems.


      
Background

      
AI is reshaping how work is organised, executed, and supervised at scale. Automation does reduce physical load, but at the same time it expands cognitive load: workers are required to keep interpreting streams of data, respond quickly to algorithmic prompts, and hold attention across many digital interfaces at once. Pauses grow thinner, tasks grow more fragmented, and productivity comes to depend on mental capacity rather than muscle.

      
From the organisational vantage point, digitally accelerated work systems also change how performance is judged. Metrics that privilege speed and output can make an organisation look effective on the surface while its adaptive capacity is quietly eroded. Fatigue signals become muted or even read as normal. This is where the paper big question is born: where do the early signs of burnout go when every performance dashboard is still green?


      
Research gap

      
The technostress and burnout literatures have long run on separate tracks. Both tend to be studied in isolation, with a dominant focus on individual coping rather than organisational antecedents. Yet the evidence shows people rarely acknowledge burnout in themselves at an early stage. Micro-signals such as shortening communication, thinning attention, and flattening emotional tone typically appear while performance still looks intact.

      
The theoretical gap is specific: nobody has theorised the intermediate condition, meaning the organisational state in which productivity holds while cognitive depletion continues. This phase exists empirically but has no theoretical name.


      
Problem statement

      
AI-mediated work systems create conditions in which visibly stable performance conceals a decline in psychological capacity. Because evaluation systems record only output and speed, organisations notice burnout only once it is expensive and hard to reverse. The problem is not merely why burnout happens, but when it begins, how long it stays hidden, and which early signals surface first.


      
Novelty

      
The principal novelty is naming and modelling a phase that has been nameless: burnout latency as a subclinical, pre-diagnostic stage between prolonged technostress and visible burnout. Two conceptual distinctions are drawn firmly. Unlike technostress, latency begins once technological pressure has already been normalised and behaviour remains functionally compliant. Unlike burnout, latency is marked by compensatory effort rather than collapse: output is maintained through increasingly expensive cognitive and emotional expenditure rather than through sustainable capacity.

      
The paper also formulates five propositions that later researchers can test, ranging from the claim that AI acceleration raises cognitive load even when formal workload holds constant, through to the claim that HR intervention during the latency phase is more effective than post-burnout recovery.


      
Theoretical landscape

      
The theoretical structure is layered along a five-stage escalation logic. First, temporal acceleration in AI-mediated work systems: algorithmic management shortens decision cycles and normalises speed as the organisational baseline. Second, cognitive load theory: working memory is finite, and AI raises both the volume and the frequency of information processing. Third, technostress as persistent cognitive and emotional pressure. Fourth, burnout as accumulated emotional exhaustion, detachment, and declining capacity.

      
That whole sequence is then positioned within the Job Demands-Resources framework. Burnout latency does not replace JD-R; it completes it. JD-R explains the imbalance of demands and resources that triggers strain, while burnout latency makes visible the temporal interval in which the health impairment process is already running but is masked by compensatory effort and steady output. HRM is positioned as the governance context that determines whether this phase is recognised early or left to accumulate.


      
Method

      
This is a conceptual paper. The model is built through a structured review and synthesis of cross-disciplinary literature on AI-mediated work tempo, cognitive load, technostress, and burnout. No empirical data were collected; the strength lies in the theoretical integration that connects constructs previously scattered across fields into a single temporal trajectory. Four devices carry it: the five-stage escalation model, a table mapping each stage to its working conditions and performance signals, a table of micro-indicators for early detection, and a conceptual vignette telling the story of an employee who appears perfectly fine.

      
Worth knowing: that vignette was not part of the original manuscript. It was written in answer to reviewer criticism that the paper was too abstract. The methodological lesson: a conceptual paper in a Q1 journal is not judged on breadth of reading but on the sharpness of a single idea that is named, modelled, given testable propositions, and translated into action.


      
Findings

      
The escalation model moves through five stages. Stage one, AI-based acceleration: tasks are system-scheduled, work is monitored in real time, performance is stable or even improving. Stage two, cognitive load accumulation: constant task switching without real pauses, performance still held but by pushing through. Stage three, technostress: skill expectations keep climbing, the boundary between work and rest thins, consistency begins to waver though still within tolerance. Stage four, burnout latency: performance is judged by output and speed, early signs go unrecognised, exhaustion proceeds in silence. Stage five, visible burnout, when the decline finally shows and the organisation only then reacts.

      
Early signs can be read across four domains, each with a reason why organisations routinely miss them. Language: messages become shorter and purely functional, but changes in communication style are almost never tracked. Attention: sustaining complex tasks becomes harder, but attention problems stay hidden as long as the work still gets finished. Emotional expression: tone flattens and warmth recedes, but a neutral manner is read as professionalism. Effort dynamics: the same output takes longer and quiet overtime creeps in, but the extra work is praised as dedication until the bill falls due.

      
The practical consequence: early detection has to rest on psychological safety and human conversation, not on intrusive surveillance.


      
Contribution

      
The theoretical contribution has four layers. First, burnout is reconceptualised as a temporal continuum uniting acceleration, cognitive load, technostress, and exhaustion in one trajectory rather than as free-standing constructs. Second, it fills the conceptual gap between chronic technostress and diagnosed burnout, a phase present empirically but unnamed. Third, it asserts an epistemic boundary that matters for HRM research: performance cannot serve as a proxy for well-being. Fourth, it gives finer temporal resolution to the health impairment pathway within JD-R, explaining why the early intervention window is routinely missed in output-oriented work systems.

      
For practice, prevention is mapped stage by stage. During acceleration, set realistic response expectations and stop rewarding always-on behaviour. As mental load accumulates, protect focus time and cut unnecessary task switching. Once technostress settles in, provide skills development support and clear recovery boundaries. And in the latency phase, treat small signals as grounds for a supportive conversation rather than grounds for monitoring or blame.

      
For public policy, the paper is a reminder that rapidly driven AI adoption needs to be balanced by protection for mental recovery, clarity about availability boundaries, and transparency about how algorithms set work tempo and evaluation, so that productivity targets do not quietly shift their cost onto workers cognitive capacity.

        Round one reviewer comments very nearly rejected
        

          
This is the most dramatic episode in the whole showcase. In round one, Reviewer 1 recommended rejection. The criticism was harsh and personal: the language was judged so complex it was hard to follow, the argument was described as lost in its own intricacy, and the manuscript was called ungrounded for offering no concrete example of what burnout actually feels like. That reviewer even wrote that the Stoic books they were reading at the time were easier to follow than this manuscript.

          
Reviewer 2 was more constructive with a major revision recommendation: the burnout latency concept had to be distinguished firmly from existing burnout process theory, particularly Maslach and Leiter; the boundary between technostress and latency needed sharpening; the escalation model was too linear and needed acknowledgement of context and moderators; novelty claims should not be overstated; and the practical implications needed intervention guidance.

          
The decision by editor Prof. Peter Stokes was the rescue: that rejection recommendation was treated as major revision, and the authors were invited to submit a revision with a point-by-point response. A door that appeared closed turned out to be open.

        

      


      

        How the criticism was answered from reject to accepted
        

          
What makes this story worth studying is that the trail of improvement can be seen directly in the published article. Reviewer 1 criticism about the absence of examples was answered by adding a conceptual vignette section: two narrative illustrations of employees who appear fine while being drained, written in everyday language. Reviewer 2 criticism about theoretical differentiation was answered with a dedicated section positioning burnout latency against its neighbouring constructs, explaining why it complements rather than replaces the JD-R framework and the Maslach-Leiter process model. The criticism about linearity was answered with a boundary conditions section acknowledging variation across work contexts.

          
After that major revision, round two still returned major revision in February 2026, round three came down to minor revision on 26 May, and one day after the final revision went in the manuscript was accepted on 28 May 2026. The lesson has three layers: a rejection recommendation is not necessarily the end of the story so long as the editor keeps the door open; the harshest criticism often points to the most real weakness, in this case language that kept the reader at a distance; and the best way to answer is not to argue in the response letter but to change the manuscript substantively.
