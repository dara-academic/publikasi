Exploring the dynamics of work satisfaction and innovation among young lecturers in post-pandemic Indonesia

Despinur Dara (first author, corresponding author) dan Saparuddin, Fakultas Ekonomi dan Bisnis, Universitas Negeri Jakarta.

In brief

      
This paper investigates the complex relationship between work autonomy, work crafting, work satisfaction, and innovative work behaviour among young Indonesian lecturers after the pandemic.

      
The most surprising finding: work autonomy turned out to have no significant effect on work satisfaction. Excessive autonomy without accompanying support can actually lower the satisfaction of young lecturers.

      
The message for higher education institutions: granting freedom alone is not enough. Young lecturers need a balance between autonomy and institutional support to stay both satisfied and innovative.


      
Background

      
Post-pandemic universities live in fast change, uncertainty, and ambiguity, and young lecturers stand at the front line. They are the ones expected to build an innovative culture amid learning technology that keeps shifting. The problem is that their appetite for innovation is often eroded by the triple load of teaching, research, and administrative duties, with limited time and funding. In those conditions the question becomes urgent: what actually keeps young lecturers satisfied and innovative, wider autonomy or something else?


      
Research gap

      
The literature still argues about which factors most influence innovative work behaviour, and evidence specific to young lecturers is almost absent. Earlier studies also tend to treat autonomy as a remedy that always works, even though findings are mixed and context dependent. This paper fills that gap by testing the chain from work autonomy through work crafting and work satisfaction to innovative behaviour within a single model, on an under-represented population, using quantitative and qualitative evidence together.


      
Novelty

      
The novelty lies in applying self-determination theory to the Indonesian academic context through a two-stage design: the statistical findings are re-examined for meaning through in-depth interviews and classroom observation. That approach lets the paper explain not only that autonomy failed to raise satisfaction but why. The informant voices reveal a darker side of autonomy that the numbers alone could not capture.


      
Theoretical landscape

      
The umbrella is self-determination theory from Deci and Ryan, which holds that motivation grows from three basic psychological needs: autonomy, competence, and relatedness. Three sequential hypotheses follow: work autonomy raises work satisfaction (H1); work crafting, meaning the proactive effort to reshape one job around one interests and expertise, raises work satisfaction (H2); and work satisfaction drives innovative work behaviour (H3). The supporting literature already carried a warning signal: crafting taken too far without institutional support can end in role ambiguity, and autonomy demands high accountability and self-discipline.


      
Method

      
The design is sequential mixed method. The quantitative stage surveyed 382 young lecturers from 33 public universities across Indonesia, from Syiah Kuala to Pattimura, using 38 indicators across 13 dimensions on a five-point Likert scale, analysed with structural equation modelling in Lisrel 8.8. The qualitative stage followed up with semi-structured interviews and direct classroom observation, coded thematically in NVivo 14, after which the two bodies of evidence were brought together through integration analysis.

      
Model fit was very good, with RMSEA of 0.019 and CFI of 0.96, and the model explained 89 per cent of the variance in work satisfaction and 72 per cent of the variance in innovative behaviour.


      
Findings

      
Two hypotheses were strongly supported: work crafting raises work satisfaction at 0.69, and work satisfaction drives innovative behaviour at 0.72. But H1 was rejected, and not merely by being non-significant: the coefficient from autonomy to satisfaction was negative at 0.13.

      
The qualitative stage explains why. One informant enjoyed the freedom to set their own schedule but often felt isolated and overwhelmed for want of university support. Another was eager to integrate technology but ran into limits of resources and time. Autonomy without scaffolding, it turns out, feels like being left alone.


      
Contribution

      
For theory, the finding shows that the need for autonomy in self-determination theory does not stand alone. Its value depends on the availability of support and resources, particularly within Indonesian academic culture.

      
For university management the prescription is concrete: build systems that balance freedom with its supports, through mentoring programmes, regular meetings, training, and access to resources, and leave room for young lecturers to craft their own work, because that is the path shown to carry satisfaction and innovation most strongly.


      
Review journey

      
This is the longest review journey in the showcase: five review rounds in the JIEB open journal system, with five editorial decisions between January and July 2024, before publication in Vol. 40 No. 1 of 2025 as article number 9972. The first round ended in major revision, and even the second round still required resubmission for a further review round. If your paper is going back and forth through revisions, know that this is normal.

        Reviewer 2, round one major revision
        

          
The second reviewer gave an assessment that was sharp but constructive. The introduction was judged good and promising. The hard criticism fell on three other sections.

          
On the literature review, the reviewer asked for deeper theoretical explanation, a more critical appraisal of earlier findings rather than mere summary, the addition of contradictory or negative findings, and rebuked a writing style judged to read like the output of an AI application.

          
On method, the reviewer asked for restructuring into research design, research context, and participants, complete with references for the interview procedure and the rationale behind each interview question.

          
On results, the reviewer asked for results to be separated from discussion, and judged a discussion resting on only one or two references to be a fundamental weakness requiring comparison against a wider set of related studies.

          
How it was answered: every point was addressed through the journal official response table, item by item, showing what was changed and where. Three lessons stand out. The discussion section is the most common weak point in a manuscript, so never treat it as a restatement of results. Major revision is not rejection but an invitation to improve. And keep the writing voice personal and critical, because reviewers are now alert to generic prose.

        

      


      

        Traces of the revision in the published paper compare against the published PDF
        

          
The four heaviest reviewer demands can be checked directly against the published article. The request to restructure the method was met exactly in the order asked: the method section is now divided into research design and context, research participants, data collection, data analysis, and instrument validation. The request for interview procedure detail and question rationale was met with four appendices, covering variable operationalisation, the questionnaire, the interview items, and the NVivo code reference.

          
The request to separate results from discussion was met by splitting them into two distinct sections, and the harshest criticism about a discussion resting on one or two references was answered with a discussion that now compares the findings against many related studies. The request to present contradictory findings was met through a literature review that now carries the opposite side: that excessive work crafting can trigger role ambiguity and work pressure, and that satisfaction pitched too high may dull the drive to keep improving.

          
An archival note: the only file retained is the response form for the second reviewer in round one, so the first reviewer comments and the dynamics of rounds two to five are not documented here. Even so, one thing reads clearly from the timeline: five review rounds are not the mark of a poor manuscript but of a journal that takes quality control seriously.
