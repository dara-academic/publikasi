Beyond digital skills: work-integrated learning, adaptability, and the thresholds for graduate work readiness

Despinur Dara (first author, Universitas Negeri Jakarta), Tuong Thien Nguyen (Foreign Trade University, Hanoi, Vietnam), dan Donny Maha Putra (Universitas Pembangunan Nasional Veteran Jakarta).

In brief

      
This paper examines how work-integrated learning, meaning learning embedded in the world of work such as internships and industry placements, shapes graduate work readiness. The key move is separating two capabilities that are usually conflated: digital literacy as an upstream enabler, and technology adaptability as the proximate driver of readiness. The paper also calculates the minimum capability threshold a student has to cross to be genuinely work ready.

      
The central message is deliberately provocative: being able to operate digital tools does not automatically make a graduate work ready. What decides it is the ability to adapt when the technology changes.


      
Background

      
The Indonesian government launched the Merdeka Belajar Kampus Merdeka policy in 2020 to bridge the lecture hall and professional practice by embedding work-integrated learning into higher education. The intent was sound; the results have been uneven. Some evaluations record smooth transitions into work, others find limited and inconsistent effects. The structural context compounds the problem: only around 19 per cent of the young population holds a higher education qualification, and fewer than 1 per cent of the workforce commands advanced digital skills such as programming, artificial intelligence, or big data analytics. The core question therefore sharpens: under what conditions does structured work experience actually prepare a graduate, rather than merely discharge an internship requirement.


      
Research gap

      
The literature has long treated digital literacy and internships as linear determinants of work readiness, as though exposure converts automatically into capability. What is overlooked is the internal transformation a person has to go through to turn exposure into capability. No study has cleanly separated the role of digital literacy as an upstream enabler from technology adaptability as the proximate driver, let alone tested whether adaptability operates as a threshold mechanism, which is precisely the question that matters most in a developing country with an uneven digital divide and inconsistent placement quality.


      
Novelty

      
The paper proposes the Adaptive Readiness Mechanism: work-integrated learning cultivates technology adaptability, that adaptability is the engine that converts learning into work readiness, and digital literacy acts as upstream scaffolding rather than a direct determinant. The methodological novelty is pairing structural equation modelling, which answers how large the effect is, with Necessary Condition Analysis, which answers a different question entirely, namely what minimum level must absolutely be crossed. The two approaches are rarely used together even though they complement each other.


      
Theoretical landscape

      
The umbrella is dynamic capabilities theory from Teece, normally applied at the organisational level, here reconceptualised at the individual level: sensing technological change, seizing new tools, and reconfiguring ways of working. Digital literacy is measured with the DigComp 2.2 framework, which now covers interaction with AI-based systems; technology adaptability is adapted from the Career Adapt-Abilities Scale; and work readiness uses the Work Readiness Scale. Eight hypotheses are derived from that framing, covering three direct paths from work-integrated learning, the path from digital literacy to adaptability, and two mediation hypotheses.


      
Method

      
A cross-sectional survey of 520 final-year students from 28 public and 19 private universities, collected across five months from December 2024 to April 2025, with a minimum of three months of work-integrated learning as the eligibility criterion. A four-point Likert scale was chosen to suppress the tendency to answer neutrally.

      
Several methodological practices here are worth copying. Data were screened for straight-lining and implausible completion times. Estimation used robust maximum likelihood with the Satorra-Bentler correction, plus WLSMV as a sensitivity check. Common method bias was addressed both procedurally and statistically. Indirect effects were tested with 5,000 bootstrap resamples. Measurement invariance was tested in stages before comparing the social science and STEM cohorts. Two open questions were also included, and the answers were used to enrich the interpretation of the numbers.


      
Findings

      
Six of the eight hypotheses were supported, and the two that were rejected carry the main message. Work-integrated learning strongly lifts digital literacy at 0.672 and contributes to adaptability at 0.303; digital literacy in turn strengthens adaptability at 0.426. But once both are modelled together, digital literacy does not predict work readiness directly at all, at 0.029 and not significant, while technology adaptability emerges as the strongest driver at 0.457. Mediation works only through the adaptability path at 0.269, while the path through digital literacy is close to zero.

      
The threshold analysis makes the point sharper still. Adaptability has to reach roughly 2.25 on a four-point scale, and that figure does not move even when the readiness target is raised, which makes it an absolute capability floor. Digital literacy needs only around 2.00, and exposure to work-integrated learning around 2.80. The pattern holds across both academic clusters.

      
Student voices in the open questions bring the numbers to life. One wrote that the system changed twice during the placement and what mattered was how fast you adapted. Another described how vendor templates never fitted, so they learned to improvise while keeping the data audit ready. One student summed it up most clearly: they already knew the basics of the software, but the real test came when the interface changed.


      
Contribution

      
Theoretically the paper carries dynamic capabilities theory out of the organisational domain and into the individual and higher education domain, while challenging the assumption of linear skill accumulation. For universities the message is blunt: placements must be designed as processes that deliberately cultivate adaptability, not merely as arrangements that supply workplace exposure, which shifts the focus from the quantity of internships to the quality of the learning experience. For employers, students should be given authentic tasks such as navigating unfamiliar platforms or resolving data mismatches, rather than routine administrative work. For policymakers, this threshold evidence calls for placement quality standards under the MBKM scheme that require mentoring, digital tasks, and problem-solving activity, because without those, widening access will not translate into real readiness.


      
Review journey

      
From the journal colophon, this paper took about three and a half months from submission to acceptance, with one round of substantial revision. That is fast for a Q1 journal, helped by positioning the contribution as a dual one: an explanatory model together with a practical threshold.

        Reviewer 1 minor revision
        

          
The first reviewer recommended minor revision with four highly technical requests: strengthen the statement of the research problem; explain the research approach in more detail including sampling, validity, and reliability; state the dimensions of every construct measured; and add goodness of fit indices to the results. Notes like these are typical for quantitative SEM-based papers. The reviewer wants readers to be able to judge measurement quality before they are asked to believe the findings.

        

      


      

        Reviewer 2 accept outright
        

          
The second reviewer recommended acceptance outright, which is rare. The praise landed squarely on the heart of the paper: the Adaptive Readiness Mechanism framework was judged a contribution both theoretical and practical, the research design was called rigorous and well structured, the results clear and consistent with the hypotheses, and the implications explicit and actionable. The Indonesian context was judged internationally relevant because it touches the universal issue of employability.

          
Beyond the two reviewers, the editor Prof. Tony Wall added conditions that first-time authors often forget: the manuscript had to be cut below 7,000 words, the revised version had to be de-anonymised, and the method section had to state ethical review or confirm alignment with institutional ethics policy. The lesson matters. A research ethics statement is not a formality; Emerald will not publish a manuscript that cannot evidence it.
