Enhancing well-being in hybrid work: the crucial role of organizational support for Indonesia's State Civil Apparatus

Despinur Dara (penulis pertama), Susan Febriantina (Universitas Negeri Jakarta), dan Suwatno Suwatno (Universitas Pendidikan Indonesia).

In brief

      
The paper that opened this whole hybrid work research line tests the effect of hybrid work, perceived organisational support, and job burnout on the well-being of Indonesian civil servants. The results form a coherent story: hybrid work turns out to raise burnout, while organisational support lowers burnout and strengthens well-being at the same time. The benefit of hybrid work for well-being only appears once organisational support moderates it.

      
One sentence carries it: hybrid work is not automatically good or bad for employees. Organisational support is what decides which way it goes.


      
Background

      
From May 2022 Indonesian civil servants who had grown used to working from home began to be called back to the office, and out of that came the hybrid arrangement combining both. The transition was not smooth: team coordination through limited virtual interaction, uneven digital competence, and often inadequate home working facilities.

      
One demographic fact makes the Indonesian context distinctive: the civil service age profile is dominated by the 51 to 60 bracket, so the potential digital divide is large. Low digital literacy does not merely slow productivity; it raises burnout risk through prolonged frustration with technology, while the loss of direct supervision blurs the boundary between work and personal life.


      
Research gap

      
Earlier hybrid work studies concentrate on the private sector and developed-country settings, focusing on generic challenges. How hybrid work affects well-being and burnout inside Indonesian government, with its particular challenges of digital literacy gaps and bureaucratic structure, remains very thinly researched. This paper fills that gap while re-testing earlier findings on fatigue from prolonged remote work on a population never previously examined.


      
Novelty

      
The novelty lies in a comprehensive approach that examines the dual impact of hybrid work in the Indonesian public sector within one complete structural model: organisational support, hybrid work, burnout, and well-being together. The paper does not start from the assumption that flexibility must be good. It deliberately leaves open the possibility that hybrid work plays a dual role, beneficial and harmful at once, depending on how it is managed. That willingness to test in both directions is what produced its most interesting finding.


      
Theoretical landscape

      
The theoretical umbrella is Herzberg dual factor theory, which reads people reactions to change as the pull between two groups of factors, supporting and inhibiting. Here the supporting factors are adequate technology, supportive organisational policy, and the benefits of flexibility; the inhibiting factors are low digital literacy, resistance to change, and concerns about life balance.

      
Six hypotheses follow: organisational support has a positive effect on hybrid work (H1) and on well-being (H2) and a negative effect on burnout (H3); hybrid work has a positive effect on well-being (H4) and a negative effect on burnout (H5); and burnout has a negative effect on well-being (H6). The constructs are measured with established instruments: the POS Questionnaire, the Flexible Work Options Questionnaire, the Maslach Burnout Inventory, and the General Health Questionnaire.


      
Method

      
The population is 978,652 central government civil servants. The Slovin technique set a target of 384 respondents, and what came back was 533 valid responses collected over three months through agency HR units.

      
Two methodological decisions here are worth studying. First, a four-point Likert scale was chosen deliberately to remove the neutral option and force respondents to take a position. Second, when the normality test showed the data were not normal, structural equation modelling estimation was switched from maximum likelihood to generalized least squares, which is more robust to assumption violations.

      
The model was tested with CB-SEM in LISREL through confirmatory factor analysis, with all loadings above threshold, composite reliability above 0.7, and good model fit at RMSEA 0.070, CFI 0.940, and GFI 0.921.


      
Findings

      
Five of six hypotheses were supported, and the one that failed is the headline. Hybrid work was predicted to reduce burnout (H5), but the data showed the opposite: the coefficient was positive at 0.196 and significant, meaning hybrid work actually raises burnout.

      
Organisational support, meanwhile, proved powerful. Its effect on well-being was the strongest anywhere in the model at 0.647, it reduced burnout at negative 0.425, and it encouraged the adoption of hybrid working. Hybrid work still contributed positively to well-being though modestly at 0.0995, and burnout eroded well-being at negative 0.162.

      
The combined message is plain: flexibility without support is a recipe for stress, and organisational support is what determines whether hybrid work ends in well-being or exhaustion.


      
Contribution

      
Theoretically the finding enriches dual factor theory by showing that the same factor, flexibility, can shift roles from supporting to inhibiting when left unmanaged, and it challenges part of the job demands-resources claim that greater job control always reduces burnout.

      
Practically the recommendations are concrete: firm working-hours policy including a right to disconnect outside working hours, adequate remote working infrastructure, well-being programmes covering stress management training and psychological counselling, and consistent communication.

      
This paper then became the foundation for two follow-up papers in this showcase: the JWAM phenomenological study that digs into meaning, and the IJOA burnout latency concept that theorises the silent phase.


      
Review journey

      
This journey teaches patience. After submission in early August 2024 the manuscript went out for review on 10 August and the outcome only arrived in mid-December, roughly four and a half months of waiting. That is normal; do not panic when the status in the system sits still for months. Once the outcome came, the rhythm reversed sharply: two revision rounds completed within three weeks across the year-end holidays, accepted on 11 January 2025 and published online on 21 January 2025. The article has now been read more than 6,500 times and cited by 7 other articles.

The revision decision came from Editor-in-Chief Prof. Daryl O'Connor with a deadline of 14 January 2025, carrying two administrative conditions typical of Taylor and Francis: a cover letter explaining the response to every reviewer comment, and every change in the manuscript marked in yellow highlight. The revised manuscript went in well before the deadline and was accepted on 11 January, three days early.


      

        Reviewer 1 focused on statistical rigour
        

          
The first reviewer praised the coherence of theory and context in the introduction, then went straight at the part that decides a paper fate: the research gap was judged not yet strong. The argument that earlier studies focused on the private sector while this one addresses the public sector was considered insufficient as a convincing gap in the literature. The literature review was also judged limited in scope, neither deep enough nor broad enough, and in need of enrichment with analysis of key studies, theories, and debates in the field.

          
The rest was a run of highly technical methodological questions. The reviewer flagged an oddity in the descriptive statistics where the maximum value was only 4 although the scale appeared to be a five-point Likert, and asked whether this was a matter of collection, coding, or interpretation. They asked for variance, kurtosis, and skewness to be added because standard deviation alone cannot establish normality. They asked which software was used for SEM and whether the approach was CB-SEM or PLS-SEM, whether exploratory and confirmatory factor analysis were run, what the values were, and whether any items were dropped for low loadings. Finally they asked for a limitations and future research section before the conclusion.

        

      


      

        Reviewer 4 focused on manuscript architecture
        

          
The fourth reviewer opened appreciatively and closed by judging the study well written and systematic, needing only some improvement. But the eight points were highly detailed about the architecture of the manuscript. The introduction was to be restructured to an explicit recipe: one paragraph introducing the topic and main concepts, then two to three paragraphs establishing the research gap, problem statement, and research objectives. All headings and subheadings were to be numbered for readability. Dual factor theory and the hypotheses were to be brought under one parent heading, and the conceptual model moved out of the method section into hypothesis development.

          
The method section was asked to answer five concrete questions: how the number of respondents was determined, how they were contacted, how bias was controlled, whether the data were self-reported, and how long collection ran and why. The discussion section was to be renamed discussion and implications, with social implications added given that the topic is employee well-being. Finally, limitations and future research were to be separated from the conclusion.

          
Interestingly this is where the two reviewers disagreed. Reviewer 1 wanted the limitations section before the conclusion; Reviewer 4 wanted it after. The authors chose to place it before the conclusion and explained that choice in the cover letter. This situation is common and worth understanding: when reviewers contradict each other, an author is not obliged to satisfy both. Choosing one and giving a reasonable justification is enough.

        

      


      

        Traces of the revision in the published paper compare against the published PDF
        

          
This page is the clearest example of how revision reshapes a manuscript, because nearly every reviewer request left a trace that can be checked directly in the published PDF. The scale oddity raised by Reviewer 1 was answered with a dedicated paragraph in the descriptive analysis explaining that the four-point scale was chosen deliberately to remove the neutral option and force respondents to take a position. The request for normality testing was answered by reporting skewness, kurtosis, and a combined chi-square value, which in turn led to an important methodological decision: because the data proved non-normal, estimation was switched to generalized least squares.

          
The software question was answered with a paragraph stating the use of CB-SEM through LISREL and the reasoning behind it. The EFA question was answered by explaining why CFA was used rather than EFA, namely because the study tests an established theoretical model rather than exploring a new structure. The request for a limitations section was met with a section of its own before the conclusion.

          
From Reviewer 4 side, heading numbering now runs cleanly from section 1 to 7, theory and hypotheses sit together under section 2, the conceptual model moved to subsection 2.3, section 5 was renamed discussion and implications complete with theoretical, practical, and policy implications, and the method section now explains the Slovin technique for sample size, contact through HR units by email with reminders, anonymisation to suppress socially desirable responding, and the reasoning for three months of data collection. The main lesson: a reviewer report that looks like an exhausting list of demands is in fact a very specific map for improvement, and answering it point by point inside the manuscript is far more persuasive than arguing in the response letter.
