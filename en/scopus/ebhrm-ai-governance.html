Linking HR policy support and AI governance to workforce adaptation: evidence from an emerging economy

Despinur Dara (first author, Universitas Negeri Jakarta) and Donny Maha Putra (Universitas Pembangunan Nasional Veteran Jakarta).

Vol. 14 No. 3 (2026), pp. 1-16 · Published 28 July 2026 · DOI: 10.1108/EBHRM-10-2025-0465 · Journal page

In brief

This paper examines how HR policy support and clarity of AI governance shape the way employees adapt to generative AI in an emerging economy. It focuses not on employees in general but specifically on those already using generative AI in their daily work.

The sharpest finding: there is a minimum level of AI dependency that has to be crossed before high adaptive performance becomes achievable. Below that threshold, no amount of support produces the result.

Drawing on survey data from 407 public and private sector employees in Indonesia, the study combines PLS-SEM with Necessary Condition Analysis, then compares the two sectors through multi-group analysis.

What it found

HR policy support and AI governance clarity are both positively related to technostress and to AI dependency. That sounds paradoxical but makes sense: the clearer the rules and the stronger the encouragement, the more deeply employees use the technology, along with the pressure that comes with it. Higher dependency, in turn, is associated with better adaptive performance.

The sector comparison shows a telling split. In the public sector, HR policy support has a stronger effect on AI dependency. In the private sector, the link between dependency and performance is the stronger one.

Theoretically, the paper brings together structuration theory and the job demands-resources model to show how HR and governance structures shape adaptation in an AI-exposed workforce, and argues that some conditions matter as prerequisites rather than as average predictors.

Three lessons sit in this timeline. The first decision took almost four months with no word in between, which is normal. Two consecutive major revisions did not mean rejection was coming; the third round dropped to minor and acceptance followed. And the author turnaround was fast: four days after the first decision, eighteen after the second, one day after the third. Of eight months in total, barely one was spent on the author side.
