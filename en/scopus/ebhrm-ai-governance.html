Linking HR policy support and AI governance to workforce adaptation: evidence from an emerging economy

Despinur Dara (first author, Universitas Negeri Jakarta) and Donny Maha Putra (Universitas Pembangunan Nasional Veteran Jakarta).

In brief

      
This paper examines how HR policy support and the clarity of artificial intelligence governance shape the way employees adapt to generative AI in an emerging economy. Its focus is not employees in general but specifically those already using generative AI in their day-to-day work.

      
The sharpest finding: there is a minimum level of AI dependency that has to be crossed before high adaptive performance becomes achievable. Below that threshold, however much support is provided, the result is unlikely to appear.

      
The paper also closes a long journey: eight months from submission on 11 November 2025, through two rounds of major revision and one minor revision, accepted on 5 July 2026 and published on 28 July 2026.


      
Background

      
Generative AI entered the workplace far faster than organisations were ready to govern it. Many agencies and companies encourage staff to use these tools yet have no clear rules on what is permitted, what is not, and who carries responsibility when something goes wrong. At the same time the HR function is asked to keep people productive amid a change in working practices that has never moved this quickly.

      
The emerging economy context adds a further layer. Technology adoption runs fast while the policy and governance apparatus catches up afterwards. This paper enters precisely that gap, drawing on data from Indonesian employees in both the public and private sectors.


      
Research gap

      
Studies of AI in the workplace generally stop at the question of whether the technology is accepted. What is rarely tested is the role of formal structures, meaning HR policy support and the clarity of governance rules, in shaping how employees adapt. Rarer still is any examination of whether some minimum condition has to be satisfied first, rather than simply an average relationship between variables.

      
The second gap lies in sector comparison. The public and private sectors face different pressures, incentives, and authority structures, yet the two are seldom tested side by side within a single model.


      
Novelty

      
The novelty lies in how the paper treats the notion of a condition. Rather than asking only how large the average effect of a factor is, it also asks whether that factor is a prerequisite that must be met first. Path analysis is therefore combined with Necessary Condition Analysis, so the results answer two questions at once: how strong the effect is, and what minimum level has to be reached.

      
The second novelty is the pairing of two frameworks rarely brought together, structuration theory and the job demands-resources model, to explain how organisational structures shape individual adaptation.


      
Theoretical landscape

      
The paper opens with a critique of the frameworks that have long dominated workplace technology research. Models such as TAM and UTAUT explain initial acceptance of a technology well, but they stop there. Neither accounts for how employees keep adapting once the technology has become part of everyday work.

      
In their place the paper draws on structuration theory from Giddens, and its central idea of the duality of structure. HR policy and AI governance rules do not sit in the background as passive scenery. They simultaneously constrain and enable employee behaviour while themselves being reshaped by employees daily practice.

      
The second layer is the job demands-resources model, which supplies the balancing logic. Policy support and rule clarity act as resources, while the demand of mastering a new technology acts as a burden. It is the combination of the two frameworks that explains why the same support can lead both to technostress and to better adaptive performance.


      
Method

      
Data were collected through an online survey in Indonesia between August and September 2024, distributed through professional and organisational networks. The target was not workers in general but employees already actively using generative AI in their work. Recruitment was purposive with sector balancing, so that the public and private sectors were equally represented.

      
Of 433 questionnaires received, 26 were removed because the respondents turned out not to be generative AI users, leaving 407 valid responses and a retention rate of 94 per cent. The composition was dominated by millennials at 52.3 per cent, followed by generation X at 35.4 per cent, generation Z at 8.8 per cent, and baby boomers at 3.4 per cent. This distribution was benchmarked against national labour market statistics so that representativeness could be measured rather than merely asserted.

      
The analysis runs in three layers. First, PLS-SEM tests the strength of relationships between constructs, answering which factors are associated with better adaptation. Second, Necessary Condition Analysis answers a different question, namely which conditions must be satisfied first. Third, multi-group analysis compares the public and private sectors. It is the combination of PLS-SEM and NCA that allows the paper to separate what merely improves an outcome from what has to be in place before it.


      
Findings

      
One thing has to be understood before reading the numbers: only two of the nine hypotheses were supported. That does not mean the other seven failed. Most of them were statistically significant; the direction simply ran opposite to what had been predicted. This is what makes the paper interesting.

      
The initial expectation was reasonable: HR policy support and AI governance clarity ought to reduce technostress and AI dependency. The opposite happened. AI governance clarity turned out to have the strongest association with technostress anywhere in the model, at 0.617, and HR policy support also raised AI dependency, at 0.272. The explanation makes sense on reflection: the clearer the rules and the stronger the support, the more deeply employees use the technology, along with the pressure that comes with it.

      
What was supported as predicted are the paths towards professional identity adaptation, both from policy support at 0.175 and from governance clarity at 0.221.

      
Towards adaptive performance, only one path genuinely works, and it works strongly: AI dependency, at 0.571. Technostress proved not to be significant at 0.052, and neither did professional identity adaptation at 0.071. What drives adaptive performance, in other words, is not how pressured someone feels or how far their identity has shifted, but how deeply they actually rely on the tool.

      
The Necessary Condition Analysis result is the most practical of all. To reach adaptive performance at a level of 3.50, AI dependency must first reach at least 3.20, with an effect size of 0.42. Below that threshold that level of performance is unattainable, however far the other factors are strengthened.

      
Two further factors act as facilitating rather than obligatory conditions: AI governance clarity with a threshold of 2.90 and HR policy support with a threshold of 2.70. Technostress is not a necessary condition at all.

      
The sector comparison produced two significant differences out of the nine paths tested. In the public sector, HR policy support drives AI dependency more strongly, at 0.332 against 0.201. In the private sector, the link between dependency and performance is the stronger one, at 0.622 against 0.493. The reading is instructive: the public sector leans more on formal policy to drive usage, while the private sector converts usage into performance more quickly.


      
Contribution

      
For organisations the prescription is concrete: role-relevant training and leadership support need to be paired with clear, usable rules on the limits of use and on responsibility. Support without rules breeds confusion; rules without support breed reluctance.

      
For scholarship, the paper shows that some factors are better treated as prerequisites than as average predictors. That shift changes the policy question from how much support to provide into what minimum level must be secured first.

      
The limitations are acknowledged openly by the authors. The design is cross-sectional and so cannot establish causal direction, and further research is recommended using longitudinal designs. The measurement of professional identity adaptation also needs sharpening, since identity work unfolds slowly and is heavily shaped by culture. Because the context is Indonesia, a collectivist setting with high power distance, cross-country comparison is recommended. Finally, since the sample consists only of employees already using generative AI, the findings apply most properly to populations already exposed to the technology.


      
Review journey

      
This is the longest journey among all the papers in this showcase: over eight months from submission to acceptance, plus another three weeks waiting on the publisher production queue. The manuscript went through two consecutive rounds of major revision and only then one round of minor revision before it was accepted. The editor in chief was Prof. Thomas Lange, with Prof. Fabian Homberg as associate editor.

        What this timeline teaches three things
        

          
First, waiting for the initial review takes a long time. The manuscript went in on 11 November 2025 and the first decision arrived on 2 March 2026, almost four months later. Nothing was heard in between. That is normal, and not a sign that anything is wrong with the paper.

          
Second, two consecutive major revisions are not the end of the world. Many authors lose heart on receiving a second major revision, feeling the manuscript is never good enough. In fact the pattern shows the editor still sees promise. The third round dropped to minor revision, and acceptance followed immediately.

          
Third, how fast the authors respond matters enormously. Look at the gaps between each editorial decision and the resubmission. After the decision of 2 March, the revision went back on 6 March, only four days. After 6 May, it went back on 24 May. After 29 June, it went back the next day and was accepted six days later. Of eight months in total, barely one was actually spent on the author side. The rest was waiting. The only part you control is how quickly you respond.

        

      


      

        Round one: three voices, one ultimatum major revision
        

          
The associate editor, Prof. Fabian Homberg, left a short but consequential note. He judged that Necessary Condition Analysis was indeed integrated into the manuscript, but only comprehensible to readers already familiar with the method. He asked for it to be explained in greater detail, including within the method section. His closing assessment was a relief: in his view every comment could be addressed through carefully considered revision.


          
The first reviewer was positive on almost every assessment criterion. Originality was judged new and useful, the literature coverage adequate, the theoretical framing appropriate, the survey design suitable, and the writing readable. The requests were about sharpening: clarify the main contribution, explain why the chosen variables best represent policy support and governance clarity, strengthen the method section with measurement quality and robustness detail, address alternative explanations and boundary conditions, and make the practical implications concrete. One sentence became the key: surprising results need a longer explanation.


          
The second reviewer was far harsher and set a make-or-break condition straight away. The opening was blunt: the central problem lay in how the sample was presented as representative of the population, and if that could not be fixed, in this reviewer view there was no way forward.


          
The criticism had two parts. First, model complexity. The model was judged unnecessarily complicated, and the reviewer suggested cutting it to three or four hypotheses. Second, and this was the threat, sample representativeness. The reviewer accepted that 407 respondents was adequate and that the near-even public and private split was sensible. But two imbalances were singled out: a male to female ratio of 64 to 36, described as troubling because the world population sits far closer to 50 to 50, and an education profile with roughly 45 per cent holding a master degree, described as more troubling still.


          
The conclusion was uncompromising: without evidence of representativeness the results could not be generalised to a population and amounted only to the opinions of 407 selected respondents. In that form, the reviewer held, the manuscript was not publishable.

        

      


      

        How the harshest criticism was answered traceable in the published paper
        

          
Answering the representativeness ultimatum. The authors did not attempt to prove the sample represented the whole Indonesian workforce, because it never could. What they did instead was redefine the target population explicitly: what the study represents is not workers in general but employees already exposed to and actively using generative AI, who are concentrated in knowledge-intensive work.


          
To evidence this, the final manuscript added a benchmark against national labour market statistics from BPS for August 2024, precisely coinciding with the data collection window. The figures are laid out openly: a working population of 144.64 million, of whom 60.81 million or 42.05 per cent hold formal employment, while university graduates from the four-year diploma through to doctorate account for only 10.84 per cent of those employed. That is exactly where the argument is built. The highly educated composition of the sample does not mirror the workforce at large, but it does mirror the knowledge-worker population where generative AI adoption is actually happening.


          
The closing sentence in the final manuscript marks the caution taken: the findings generalise to AI-exposed employees in knowledge-intensive work within an emerging economy, while generalisation to the wider workforce is not claimed. The criticism was answered not by rebuttal but by narrowing the claim until it was exactly the size of the evidence.


          
Answering the request to cut hypotheses. Here the authors chose not to comply, and that is a decision worth recording. The final manuscript retains all nine hypotheses in a structure of two levers acting on three mechanisms and then on one outcome. The reasoning is stated openly in the paper: the three mechanisms are non-redundant and represent distinct routes of adaptation, namely strain while learning, functional reliance while using, and role identity adjustment. Collapsing them into fewer hypotheses would obscure those distinct routes. A reviewer may recommend, an author may argue, and the editor weighs it up.


          
Answering the associate editor. The explanation of Necessary Condition Analysis was expanded down to its basic concept: that a necessary condition works like a bottleneck, so that in its absence no amount of any other factor will produce the outcome. The effect size thresholds used were added, along with an explanation that PLS-SEM and NCA answer two different questions and are therefore complementary.

        

      


      
One detail worth noting as a lesson: the gap between acceptance and publication is not trivial. This manuscript was accepted on 5 July but only became readable to the public on 28 July. Accepted does not yet mean published, and that gap needs allowing for if you are working to a promotion or reporting deadline.
