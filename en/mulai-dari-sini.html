Not sure where to begin? Pick the one that describes you

This site holds a lot of material, and reading it in the wrong order wastes your time. Below are three paths. Choose the one that matches where you actually are, and follow the four steps in order.

I have never submitted a manuscript at all

You have research or data but no idea where to begin. These four steps are deliberately in an order that should not be shuffled.

1 Learn the vocabulary firstBefore anything else, understand the terms journals use. Without that, an editor decision letter reads like a foreign language and you will misinterpret it. 2 Choose the right journalFour access models, how to read quartiles, and the warning signs of a predatory journal. Choosing badly here costs months, and sometimes money. 3 Follow one process end to endThe smoothest journey in this showcase: minor revision, then accepted. Useful for building a realistic picture before you live through it yourself. 4 Get the paperwork readyA pre-submission checklist, so the manuscript is not rejected at the editor desk over technicalities that were easy to avoid.

My manuscript is under review or has just come back for revision

You are past the hardest part, which is starting. What matters now is how you answer.

1 Make sure you understand the decisionMajor revision, minor revision, and revise and resubmit mean very different things, and misreading them leads either to needless panic or misplaced calm. 2 Read real reviewer commentsEight lessons drawn from actual reviewer letters, including the harshest. Seeing that others are criticised hard is both reassuring and instructive. 3 Study a case with two major revisionsIncluding how a reviewer threat over sample representativeness was answered without arguing back, and why the authors refused to cut their hypotheses. 4 Draft your response letterA sample response to reviewers and a point-by-point response table, ready to fill in. On the second round this document often matters more than the manuscript.

My manuscript was rejected

The part people talk about least, even though nearly every researcher has been through it. Rejection is data, not a verdict.

1 Read a case that was nearly rejected and still publishedOne reviewer recommended rejection in the first round, calling the manuscript too abstract. It was published in a Q1 journal after revision. 2 Check whether the journal was the mistakeMost fast rejections are about fit rather than research quality. Moving the manuscript to the right journal often works better than rewriting it. 3 Recognise the recurring criticismsThe five things reviewers flag most often, from a discussion thin on references to a missing research ethics statement. 4 Rebuild the argumentHow the harshest criticism gets used to strengthen a manuscript rather than merely patched over.

None of these fit your situation? Write to dara@unj.ac.id and describe where you are stuck.
