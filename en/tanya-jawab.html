Cost and access

Do you have to pay to publish in a Scopus journal?

No. This is the misconception that most often stops people before they try. Of the seven works in this showcase, six Scopus papers and one SINTA journal, only one incurred an article processing charge. The other six cost nothing.

There are four journal access models and only one requires the author to pay. Two papers here went through the subscription route, one through the hybrid route without paying, and three through open access journals that charge no fee at all.

Read about the four access models →

If it is open access, does that not always mean paying?

Not always. There is a set of journals whose costs are already covered by the institution behind them, usually a university, a ministry, or the publisher itself. The article is open to everyone while the author is asked for nothing.

These journals rarely sit at the top of a search result, so they have to be sought out on purpose. Three works in this showcase were published that way.

Dealing with review outcomes

My paper came back with major revision. Does that mean it will be rejected?

No. Major revision means the manuscript is judged promising but has fundamental issues to resolve first. It will be reassessed after you revise.

Receiving major revision twice in a row is still normal. The Evidence-based HRM paper in this showcase went through two rounds of major revision, then one minor, before it was accepted.

See the case →

One reviewer recommended rejection. Is there still hope?

There is, so long as the editor has not closed the door. The final decision rests with the editor, not the reviewer.

On the burnout latency paper here, one reviewer openly recommended rejection with painful criticism. The editor treated that as major revision and invited the authors to revise. The paper is now published in a Q1 journal.

Read the case from reject to accepted →

Two reviewers gave contradictory advice. Which one should I follow?

You are not obliged to satisfy both. Choose one, then explain your reasoning courteously in the cover letter.

This genuinely happened on the Cogent Psychology paper here. One reviewer wanted the limitations section before the conclusion, the other wanted it after. The authors chose one, explained why, and the paper was accepted.

See the other seven lessons →

My manuscript was desk rejected. What now?

Do not immediately rewrite the substance. A desk reject is usually not about research quality but about fit with the journal scope, language that is not yet readable, or formatting that ignores the guidelines.

Check first whether your topic really falls within that journal scope. If not, moving the manuscript to a better-matched journal often works better than rewriting it.

Timelines and procedure

How long does it usually take from submission to publication?

It varies enormously between journals, and that is not a reflection of your manuscript quality. The range in this showcase is wide. The fastest was accepted in five weeks; the slowest took five review rounds across almost a year.

What needs preparing mentally is the wait for the first decision. On the Evidence-based HRM paper the manuscript went in on 11 November and the first decision arrived on 2 March, nearly four months with no word at all. That is normal.

See what is currently under review →

How long should I wait before asking the editor about my manuscript status?

Check the submission system first. Almost every major publisher shows live status, so you do not need to write merely to find out where things stand.

If the status has not moved at all for more than four months, it becomes reasonable to send a short, courteous note to the editor. Asking earlier rarely speeds anything up.

Can I submit the same manuscript to two journals at once?

No. Submitting the same manuscript to more than one journal simultaneously breaches publishing ethics, and nearly every journal asks you to declare in writing that the work is not under review elsewhere.

If discovered, the manuscript can be withdrawn from both journals and the record stays attached to your name. Wait for one decision to conclude first.

Choosing a journal

How can I tell whether a journal is predatory?

There are ten warning signs to check and four verification steps, all free and taking under ten minutes.

The easiest signs to spot: soliciting by email with excessive praise, promising publication within days, fees only mentioned after acceptance, and a scope that spans every field at once.

See the ten signs and how to check →

What is the difference between Q1, Q2, Q3 and Q4, and how do I check?

Quartiles divide the journals in a field into four groups by citation performance. Q1 is the top quarter, Q4 the bottom.

Two things are widely misunderstood. One journal can sit in different quartiles in different subject fields, so always state the category. And quartiles change every year. Check directly at scimagojr.com rather than relying on claims on the journal own site.

Read how to interpret quartiles →

Is a national SINTA journal better, or should I go straight to international?

The question is not national or international, but whether the journal runs genuine peer review.

A good national journal has three irreplaceable roles. It is a place to practise under more manageable pressure. It reaches the right readers, because research on Indonesian policy often lands harder when read by Indonesian policymakers. And some career tracks genuinely require it.

See the SINTA 2 paper →

My sample is small. Can it still reach a Q1 journal?

Yes. The hybrid work paper here used only ten informants and was published in a Q1 journal. Reviewers did flag the sample limitation, but the manuscript still went through.

Two things make the difference. The contribution claimed has to be proportionate to the evidence you hold, and the limitations have to be acknowledged openly. What is non-negotiable is not sample size but clarity of theoretical framing and transparency of procedure.

Preparing the manuscript

Which section do reviewers criticise most often?

The discussion. This is the number one complaint across every review document in this showcase.

The most frequent mistake is writing the discussion as a restatement of results, or resting it on only one or two references. The discussion is where your findings are set against the findings of others, so prepare that section from the outset.

See the five most common criticisms →

Is a research ethics statement really compulsory?

It is, and it is what first-time authors most often forget. The editor of Higher Education, Skills and Work-Based Learning stated plainly that the journal does not publish manuscripts that cannot evidence alignment with institutional ethics policy.

Prepare the ethical review statement while you are writing the method section, not when it is demanded during revision.

Before you write, please note

  • A reply can take one to two weeks, since these are answered around teaching and research.
  • Requests to be added as a co-author on work not undertaken together from the outset are not accepted.
  • Full manuscripts are not reviewed. A specific question about one section is far more likely to be answered than a request to read a whole paper.
  • Translation and language editing are not offered, whether paid or unpaid.

These limits are stated openly not to turn anyone away, but so that nobody waits for a reply that was never going to come.
